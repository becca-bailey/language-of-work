I built two new instruments this month and pointed them at the same nineteen companies. The
first is a **craft axis**: an embedding contrast between craft language ("we take the time
to get the details right") and iteration language ("we release small changes continuously
and let real usage tell us what to fix") — both positive self-presentations, measuring
which attractor a careers page sits nearer. The second is an **AI mention tracker**: the
share of a company's careers-page text that mentions AI at all, which sat under 2% for two
decades and then jumped to 12–13% in 2025–26.

The question I actually wanted to ask: do they move in opposite directions? When a company
starts talking about AI, does it stop talking about craft?

**The honest answer is that the data can't say yet — and what it can say points somewhere
more specific.** Across nineteen companies, the correlation between the change in craft
language and the change in AI language is indistinguishable from zero (Spearman ρ = +0.09,
p = .72), and so is the correlation between the two levels (ρ = −0.11, p = .66). That's
not surprising: AI language has existed on careers pages for barely three years, and
nineteen companies is a small panel. A trade-off, if there is one, needs more time to show
up in motion.

But it may already show up in *position*.

<CraftAiScatterViz />

Look at the corners. The upper region — companies whose recent careers copy mentions AI in
a fifth to a quarter of everything they publish — is Salesforce, Google, and (on a thin
sample) Meta. None of them is craft-coded; all three sit in the middle of the craft axis.
And the right edge — the companies whose language sits hardest on the craft pole — is
Apple and Starbucks, and both are nearly AI-silent: Apple mentions AI in about 3.5% of its
recent chunks against an industry average of 12–13%, and Starbucks in none at all. Apple
is the sharpest case, because its silence is not a coverage artifact — its corpus is the
best-shaped in the whole project, continuous from 2005, over a hundred chunks a year
recently. The company that talks most about taking time, polish, and durability simply
does not talk about AI to job candidates.

One reading is that this is the trade-off, visible in levels before it's visible in
trends: craft identity and AI enthusiasm are two different employer brands, and companies
have already chosen one. But the data offers a live counterexample to any strong version
of that claim, and it deserves equal billing.

A note on the mismatched units in this chart: craft is plotted as a within-company
z-score because its raw value is a cosine projection whose absolute scale means nothing —
what's interpretable is whether a year is unusually craft-y *for that company*. AI stays
a raw share because it has what a proportion should have — a true zero and readable units
("13% of chunks") — and because z-scoring a signal that was ~0 for fifteen years would
turn any single mention into a spike. The scatter above makes the opposite choice, using
raw craft levels, since comparing *across* companies is exactly what within-company
z-scores erase. The question this chart asks — do the two lines move in opposite
directions? — is directional, and direction doesn't care about units.

**Salesforce's craft language went *up* while its AI language exploded** — from roughly
+0.05 to +0.08 on the craft axis across the same three years its AI share went from ~1% to
25%. Whatever Salesforce is doing, it is not swapping one vocabulary for the other; it's
running both. The dashboard also holds two odd cases worth watching rather than
interpreting. Basecamp — the company I added to this corpus specifically as the
anti-hustle, craft-coded counterpoint — drifted *off* the craft pole in 2024–26 (from
+0.037 to +0.009) while mentioning AI exactly zero times in 163 chunks. And GitHub and
GitLab, the only two companies whose careers language sits on the *iteration* pole, are
also nearly AI-silent — GitHub, the company that ships Copilot, does not mention AI in a
single captured careers chunk from 2024–26. Careers pages are clearly not a mirror of
product strategy; they're a separate register with its own inertia.

Three caveats travel with every chart here. Meta's recent AI share (over half of captured
chunks) rests on a thin corpus — all but a handful of its captured 2026 chunks mention AI,
but "a handful" is the operative phrase. Nvidia's apparent trajectory is unusable in the
middle: 2019 and 2021–23 are coverage holes in the archive, and the hole sits exactly on
the deep-learning boom. And Uber's post-2020 years are similarly unmeasurable until
manual capture fills them.

What would settle the question: two or three more years of careers pages, the archive
holes filled for Nvidia and Uber, and the tool-versus-mandate framing axis — which today
finds even Salesforce's dense AI copy balanced between "AI as something we give you" and
"AI as something we expect of you." If the mandate era everyone can feel in memos ever
reaches the careers page, this is where it should first become visible — and the craft
holdouts are the companies to watch when it does.
