I also wanted to study diversity and inclusion language — how often companies mention it, and
what kind of signal it is. Is it vague and aspirational, or does it name specific goals for
specific groups? (Mostly, when it existed at all, it was vague and aspirational.)

DEI language isn't the same as policy; it's a signal, not proof of good conditions for
marginalized people. But as a job seeker, it's the kind of signal that decides whether I
apply. So I classified every careers-page chunk into a *register* — a type of DEI language —
with the Claude API, hand-tuning the prompts. Early on it couldn't tell "we want a diverse
workforce" from "we serve a diverse customer base"; the difference matters. Amazon's early
diversity mentions were almost always about customers — its first real *workforce* language
shows up around 2016 ("a company of builders who bring varying backgrounds … gender, race, age,
national origin, sexual orientation"), and only in 2026 does it harden into a measurable claim:
"we track the representation of our workforce."

The chart stacks each company's registers by year: inclusion language above the line,
counter-programming below — apolitical / anti-DEI framing and the "civilizational mission"
language best exemplified by Palantir's many invocations of "the West."

## About "civilizational mission"

One counter-programming category needs its own explanation. The chart marks as
*civilizational mission* the chunks where a careers page frames the employer's identity
around defending or ensuring the future of "the West." In this corpus that is Palantir's
language, and only recently: "Palantirians deliver mission-critical outcomes for the
West's most important institutions" (2025); "We built Palantir to ensure the future of
the West, not to tinker at the margins" (2026). The category requires the explicit
invocation — generic defense-industry copy about deterrence, battlefields, or "the
world's most important institutions" does not count, even from the same company. The
label is descriptive: it records that this vocabulary appeared on a hiring page,
nothing more.

But the vocabulary itself is not neutral, and it would be evasive to present it as if it
were. "Defending Western civilization" is a load-bearing phrase in contemporary far-right
rhetoric: it is the frame the European Identitarian movement
[organizes around](https://www.populismstudies.org/Vocabulary/identitarians/), the banner
of its ["Defend Europe" campaigns](https://www.sv.uio.no/c-rex/english/news-and-events/right-now/2019/politics-through-the-media.html),
and the phrase Rep. Steve King placed in a now-infamous series — "White nationalist,
white supremacist, Western civilization — how did that language become offensive?" —
that [critics read as saying the quiet part into a bullhorn](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/nbcblk/rep-steve-king-crossed-line-race-using-bullhorn-not-dog-n959161)
([the Washington Post's analysis](https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/2019/01/15/steve-king-says-he-was-just-defending-western-civilization-thats-racist-too/)
argued the "Western civilization" defense was itself the tell). Separately, scholars like
Kwame Anthony Appiah have argued the concept is a
[modern political construction](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2016/nov/09/western-civilisation-appiah-reith-lecture),
built to draw a line around an "us" — which is precisely what makes it useful as an
audience signal.

None of this makes every use of "the West" a dog whistle. Palantir's CEO has published a
bestselling book — [*The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future
of the West*](https://sites.prh.com/technologicalrepublicpressrelease) — arguing openly
for this framing as national-security philosophy, and
[critics engage it on those terms](https://newrepublic.com/article/191786/alex-karps-war-west-palantir).
But a careers page is an audience-selection device, and the substitution the data shows
is meaningful: where peer companies' hiring pages said "everyone belongs here," this one
[now says the future of the West is at stake](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/palantir-posted-a-manifesto-that-reads-like-the-ramblings-of-a-comic-book-villain-181947361.html).
Whether a given reader hears earnest defense-tech branding or a civilizational
dog-whistle — and those readings are not mutually exclusive — the chart's claim stays
narrow: this language appeared, on these pages, in these years, while the
belonging-era vocabulary went away.

## How this was measured

Each careers-page chunk is embedded and classified into a DEI register by an LLM, with
manually tuned prompts. The inclusion axis measures topic, not direction — anti-DEI copy ("a
refuge from division") scores high because it borrows belonging vocabulary — so the register
classifier, not a single score, is what surfaces the flip. In the chart, a solid baseline
tick marks a page that was archived but silent on DEI; a dashed tick marks a year with no
archived page at all.

Two honest limits on the counter-programming bars. First, the classifier's counter
categories are calibrated on the known examples (Coinbase's apolitical memo, Palantir's
civilizational framing), so a company expressing opposition in genuinely novel language
could be missed — read the counter counts as a floor, not a census. Second, the
"civilizational mission" category is, in this corpus, a single company's rhetoric:
every chunk in it is Palantir's. It measures a deliberate repositioning of employer
branding that displaced DEI-era language — a claim about rhetoric, not about any
company's internal employment practices. Companies mostly retreat from DEI language by
deletion and replacement, not announcement, which is why the retreat shows up in this
chart as shrinking bars and rising apolitical framing rather than explicit rollback
statements.
