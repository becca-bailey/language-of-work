In 2009, Netflix published a [125-slide deck](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/culture-2009/8469957) on how it ran its culture. Sheryl Sandberg called
it the most important document ever to come out of Silicon Valley. It defined a
performance-filter culture in unusually blunt terms — and defined "performance" with no metric
at all.

<DeckQuotes />

## What traveled, and what stayed home

I went looking for that language on other companies' careers pages — embedding each Netflix
idea and its paraphrases, then flagging any company sentence that came close. Most of the deck
never left the building. But a few ideas did, and the lineage is legible: Coinbase lifted the
severance line almost word-for-word; Meta turned the "no rules" ethos into "we have values,
not rules."

<QuoteLineage />

The harsh mechanics — the keeper test, "a team, not a family," context-not-control — stayed
Netflix's own. What spread wasn't the cruelty; it was the softer, deniable version. (Phrases
like "raise the bar" and "only the best" show up everywhere, but those are industry
convergence — Amazon's, not Netflix's — so I don't count them as propagation.)

## The scoreboard that isn't there

All of it borrows the language of objective performance, and none of it defines how that
performance is measured. Across roughly 1,500 culture chunks in this corpus, the copy invokes
merit again and again — and not once names a metric.

Netflix's deck even refuses to keep score — "we have no bell curves or rankings or quotas such
as 'cut the bottom 10% every year'" — while demanding you be a "high performer" judged by a
manager's gut, the keeper test. It borrows sports' objectivity without its scoreboard, and the
discretion that fills the gap is where bias enters.
