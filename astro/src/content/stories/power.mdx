## How this was measured

- Every line is a mean over the same nineteen companies, all scored on all
  four axes — no per-metric subsetting. The three companies added mid-2026
  (Uber, Apple, Nvidia) have thinner historical coverage, Uber especially.
- Which counterforce (idealism or DEI) tracks the cycle more closely is
  not a stable result — the lead changed at eleven, thirteen, and nineteen
  companies — so nothing hangs on it. An earlier draft's much starker DEI
  number was an artifact of incomplete data (one company's mid-decade DEI
  language unclassified and counted as zero); these correlations are
  supporting texture, not the finding.
- What separates performance from the counterforces is amplitude and
  unanimity, not its differenced correlation: its total swing is three to
  six times smaller than idealism's or DEI's, and firm-by-firm it's a coin
  flip (10 of 19 positive, median ≈ 0) where idealism and DEI lean clearly
  positive (16 and 15 of 19). Performance never surged, so there was no
  concession to revoke.
- Wellbeing is measured differently and read more cautiously: an embedding
  axis (balance-and-rest vs. intensity-and-sacrifice) on mission-page
  chunks. Its 2020 spike is the largest single-axis jump in the
  fingerprint set (z ≈ +2.1). Its differenced correlation with quits is
  negative (−0.25, driven by 2020, when quits fell and care language
  exploded) — the claim is precisely that wellbeing answered the pandemic
  emergency, not the bargaining table. The locus shift rests on 78–194
  benefit chunks a year, and nearly half the wellbeing company-years sit
  on fewer than five mission chunks: two imperfect instruments pointing
  the same direction, not one clean measurement. The receipts live in
  [the wellbeing story](/stories/wellbeing).
- One honest confound runs through 2026: the DEI retreat coincides with
  both fading worker leverage and a political backlash, and this data
  cannot separate the two. What it can show is that the worker-serving
  language is the part that got cut.
