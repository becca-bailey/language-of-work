All of this raises a harder question. If careers-page language is mostly a reflection of the
labor market, what does it say about worker power?

To find out, I overlaid three language trajectories — idealism, DEI, and performance — against
the JOLTS quit rate from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, a rough proxy for when workers
had the leverage to walk. It's not an exact science, but the patterns are there.

<PowerChart />

Netflix has always kept a high performance bar, but its idealism drops right before the quit
rate does. Across all eleven companies the pattern is softer but holds. **DEI** — the
worker-oriented language — rises and recedes with worker power even after you strip out the
decade's shared upward trend; it's the one signal that genuinely responds to leverage rather
than just riding the boom. **Idealism** climbs with the same boom but mostly co-trends —
workers were always a little cynical about billionaires promising to change the world. And
**performance**, which serves whoever can hire and fire, stays flat near the top no matter
what.

In 2026 you can't trust what a company says about itself. But you can trust the pattern of
when it stops saying it. Cultural signaling doesn't give workers power — it reflects the power
they already have.

## How this was measured

Each line is an industry mean over the same eleven companies (2013–2026), every one scored on
all three axes — no per-metric subsetting. The quits rate is economy-wide, a proxy for tech
bargaining power; read this as co-movement, not causation.

The DEI link is the most robust result: it survives first-differencing (r ≈ +0.43) and
leave-one-company-out (+0.60 to +0.75), and held steady when Meta's history was substantially
re-expanded. Idealism's raw correlation (r ≈ +0.59) is mostly shared trend, so read it as
co-trending, not power-responsive. At six companies the aggregate was too noisy to read; it
only firmed up at eleven, so treat it as suggestive, not decisive.

One axis is missing on purpose. Wellbeing — the balance-and-rest framing that's the clearest
*worker concession* — lives in benefits copy, not mission statements, so this careers corpus
under-measures it badly. It belongs with the benefits data, where I'll come back to it.
