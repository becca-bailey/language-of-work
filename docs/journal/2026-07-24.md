# July 24, 2026

The good news about biased training data is that it can reflect our biases back to us. When I talk about "masculine-coded" or "feminine-coded", I want to be clear that this isn't based on a personal judgement like "direct communication is for men, therefore companies shouldn't talk about direct communication". Instead, I am highlighting the computed proximity to masculinity. If the training data has these concepts mapped to statements made by men, for men, or about men, the embedding will show that.

And it's not just DEI or anti-DEI language. DEI language is often linked to femininity according to this embedding calculus, but Basecamp is an interesting example. According to my DEI measurements, Basecamp barely registers. Yet it ranks second-highest for masculine-coded language, most likely because of how much their language has been shaped by the opinions and preferences of their vocal male founders.

The other bit of language I want to find a way of calling out is exclusionary language like "we aren't for everyone" or "we are only for people who want to achieve greatness" or whatever the exact quotes are. This is interesting because it sits in such stark contrast with feminine-coded inclusion language, and I wonder how much of this is intentionally included to appeal to a certain kind of men. I'm still not sure how to represent this with data.

Like, is there a way not just to measure which concepts are masculine, but which concepts are anti-feminine? Would that measurement be different?

I like the idea of looking closely at the "not" statements and measuring the things they are against against the embedding pole. Like "we're not for everyone" -> "for everyone" -> this will most likely be a feminine-coded concept.

What I really want to know, but is outside the scope of this particular study, is whether masculine-coded language is correlated with real gender inclusion outcomes.
